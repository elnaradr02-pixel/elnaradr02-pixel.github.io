How I solve problems

01

Alfa Z learning platform — from the database schema to the student dashboard

Problem

The school needed a system that controls learning, not a shelf of video lessons. There are no cohorts: every student starts on their own day and follows a personal schedule, so nobody notices a child has stalled unless the system computes the lag itself. Off-the-shelf LMSs cannot do that, and they know nothing about curators, lesson-based subscriptions or a parent who needs a WhatsApp message.

Solution

Designed and built the platform from scratch as a full-stack product: Next.js 15 + TypeScript, PostgreSQL + Prisma, Auth.js, background jobs on pg-boss, email via Resend, Web Push and WhatsApp notifications, Sentry, Docker deploys via GitHub Actions to a VPS. Inside: four roles with an access matrix, a lesson unlock schedule from the start date with pauses and freezes, automatic lag calculation and an escalation ladder for curators, points, streaks and a monthly leaderboard, quizzes with a question bank and safe regrading, payment tracking with access blocking, certificates, a CodeMirror lesson editor with sandboxed rendering and 43 mini-games inside lessons. Courses exist in parallel in Russian and Kazakh.

Result

The platform is live at platformalfa-z.kz and already in use by the school: students take lessons, submit homework and earn points, and the founder has signed off on the result. Invite-only access, RU / KZ interface, dark theme, PWA with push notifications. Curators see lag-based risk flags, parents get reports and payment reminders in WhatsApp, admins get a full audit log. The whole path from spec to release was walked by one person with AI agents: 130+ commits and 113 test files in a month.

02

Alfa-Z school website — a distinctive design instead of the competitors' template

Problem

I studied competing IT schools' websites and noticed they all share one visual formula: green, purple, neon, blue, black. The sites look interchangeable, are padded with filler text and often work poorly on mobile.

Solution

Designed and built a site with an unusual palette (midnight, coral, cream) I had not seen at any competitor. Cut the filler: only concrete information about what kids do on the courses, with work samples on the first screen. Tested on mobile and desktop with no jank.

Result

Launched July 16, 2026, published on GitHub, stable on every device. A parent understands within seconds what their child will be doing, without calling a manager.

03

Trial-lesson builder — personalisation instead of a boring Zoom class

Problem

The standard trial lesson is an hour on Zoom where the child just repeats after the teacher. It is dull and sells the course poorly: the parent sees no tangible result, the child is not engaged.

Solution

Built an HTML page with an embedded site builder. The child enters their name, age, interests and dream profession, uploads a photo of family, a pet or a favourite place, picks a structure and a theme, and walks away with their own mini portfolio site. The page also holds interactive samples of each of the school's five tracks (a Unity mini-game, a Caesar cipher demo for the algorithms course) so the child feels what the course is about, plus a five-question quiz that suggests the right track.

Result

The finished site becomes something to show off to the family, which does most of the sales team's job before a manager ever calls. The trial lesson has grown into a standalone web app (alfa-z-trial-lesson.vercel.app): four steps, RU / KZ, dark theme, a mentor sign-in.

04

Interactive lesson format — engagement and retention through simulators

Problem

A typical course is a video, a wall of text and a confusing homework task. Students get stuck on homework and often stop showing up.

Solution

Designed and built a new lesson format in HTML/CSS/JS with animations (“Blue Reflect”), where the colour scheme changes per lesson. Under each topic there is a live simulator matching the material: for the Telegram bot lesson, a working bot simulator you can poke before writing any code. Homework comes in two levels (hard, and an easier fallback), each with a hidden cheat sheet so one tough task does not make a student quit. Every lesson ends with a 7–8 question quiz with explanations and flip-to-reveal memory cards.

Result

The format reduces drop-off on hard topics and speeds up retention through hands-on practice and repetition via cards and quizzes.
